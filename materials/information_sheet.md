# Information sheet (draft for ethics review)

**Study:** Can students fool classroom attention software?
**Researcher:** Malak Zubair, Assistant Professor of Computer Science, G.C.M.S Timergara; PhD student, University of Malakand.

## What is this study about?

Some schools use cameras and software that claim to tell whether students are paying attention. We want to find out how easily such software can be fooled, and whether it treats some students unfairly. You will help by watching short lecture videos and, in some parts, by **acting**: looking attentive when you are thinking about something else, or looking distracted while you are really learning.

## What will I do?

- Sit at a laptop (or in your normal classroom seat) for about one hour.
- Watch ten short videos. Before each one you get an instruction card.
- In some parts you memorise a list of words; afterwards you write down the words you remember.
- After each video you answer five short quiz questions.

## What is recorded?

The laptop camera is used, but **no picture or video of you is saved**. The software turns each camera frame into a few numbers (for example, the angle of your head, where your eyes point, when you blink) and throws the picture away immediately. Your quiz answers and the number of words you remembered are also saved. Everything is stored under a participant number, never your name.

You may tell us, if you wish, whether you wear glasses, whether your face is partly covered, and whether you usually look down when listening to a teacher. This helps us check whether the software is unfair to some students. You do not have to answer.

## Do I have to take part?

No. Taking part or not has **no effect on your marks or on how your teachers treat you**. You can stop at any time without giving a reason, and you can ask for your numbers to be deleted at any time before the results are published.

## Risks and benefits

There are no known risks beyond the mild effort of concentrating for an hour. You may find it interesting to see how the software works. The results may help protect students from unfair software in the future.
